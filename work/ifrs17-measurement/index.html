All case studies US industry reserves, IFRS 17

Discounting makes a reserve smaller. On one line, IFRS 17 makes it bigger

US$25.16bn of US industry claims reserves remeasured the way IFRS 17 requires: discounted for the time until payment, then loaded with an explicit risk adjustment. Five of six lines fall. Product liability rises 1.8%, because its uncertainty costs more than its waiting time saves.

ReserveUS$25.16bn
Lines6
Valued at31 December 1997
ToolsPython, numpy
Data

CAS Loss Reserving Database, the triangles bundled with the chainladder Python package: NAIC Schedule P cumulative paid losses for all reporting US insurers, summed by line, accident years 1988 to 1997.

Method

Volume-weighted chain ladder to ultimate and the future payment pattern; present value at a flat rate, mid-year; risk adjustment from an over-dispersed Poisson bootstrap of the same triangle (5,000 simulations with process variance), taken as a percentile less the mean and discounted on the same pattern.

Why it matters

A claims reserve used to be one number

Under the old basis an insurer carried outstanding claims at what it expected to pay, undiscounted, with any prudence inside the estimate. IFRS 17 splits that into the present value of future cash flows plus a risk adjustment for non-financial risk. Two of those pieces are choices as much as calculations: the discount rate, and what uncertainty is worth. So the useful output is not one number but how far the number moves across defensible choices, and on which line.

Step one

Discounting is a question of the payment pattern

At a flat 5.7% with a mid-year convention, the six lines fall from US$25.16bn to US$23.02bn: US$2.14bn comes off before any judgement is applied. The mean time to payment runs from 1.47 years for private motor to 2.29 years for medical malpractice, and that alone sets the order in which discounting shrinks the lines.

Step two

The same three words are worth US$819m or US$2.07bn

IFRS 17 does not prescribe the risk adjustment; it requires the confidence level to be disclosed. Here the uncertainty comes from bootstrapping the same triangles: 5,000 resampled refits with process variance. At the 75th percentile the six lines need US$819m; at the 95th, US$2.07bn. Both are defensible, so the confidence level is the number, not a footnote.

The spread differs by line. Private motor's simulated reserve has a coefficient of variation of 3.8%; product liability's is 24.6%, with a long right tail.

Adding the six risk adjustments assumes every line goes wrong at once. Treating them as independent gives US$485m instead of US$819m at the 75th percentile. That one assumption is worth US$334m, 41% of the risk adjustment, and belongs in the disclosure.

Step three

Five lines fall. One rises

Product liability is the exception: its risk adjustment is 15.0% of the reserve while discounting saves only 11.5%. Every other line's uncertainty is cheaper than its waiting time.

The book as a whole comes down 5.5%. Reported alone, that says IFRS 17 releases reserves. On one line it does the opposite, and it is the line where the estimate is least reliable.

  • ✓

    The bootstrap reproduces the reserve it measures. The mean of the simulations is within 0.35% of the chain ladder reserve on 5 of the 6 lines. If it were not, the spread would describe the simulation rather than the risk.

  • !

    Product liability is 2.4% high, and that is reported rather than smoothed. Its triangle has negative incremental payments from recoveries, and its simulated distribution is strongly skewed. The line is flagged as the least reliable of the six.

  • ✓

    Residuals are scaled and centred, with over-dispersion fitted per line. A single shared parameter would have made motor look riskier and product liability safer than either is.

Limits

What this analysis cannot tell you

  • No tail beyond development year 10. Durations, reserves and the discount effect are all understated, most of all for the liability lines.
  • A flat discount rate, not a yield curve. IFRS 17 asks for rates consistent with the cash flows; the flat rate is a stated simplification.
  • One risk adjustment technique. A bootstrap percentile is common, not required. Cost of capital or a stress approach would give other answers from the same data.
  • Paid data only. No case estimates, claims handling costs, reinsurance or expenses in the fulfilment cash flows.
  • US industry data from 1997. The point is the measurement mechanics on real claims development, not a statement about any insurer.
